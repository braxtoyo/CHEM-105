CATEGORY 1: ELECTRON CONFIGURATIONS AND MAGNETISM
TOPIC: THREE FILLING RULES
Aufbau: fill the lowest available energy orbitals first.
Pauli: each orbital holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins. No two electrons in an atom can have all four quantum numbers identical.
Hund: fill equal-energy orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing.
For three p orbitals, place one electron in each before adding a second electron to any orbital.
TOPIC: FILLING ORDER
Common filling sequence: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p.
Capacities: s holds 2 electrons. p holds 6. d holds 10. f holds 14.
This sequence is a basic filling guide. Actual configurations have exceptions, and orbital energies vary with the atom or ion.
TOPIC: COUNT ELECTRONS FIRST
Neutral atom: electron count equals atomic number.
Positive ion: subtract electrons equal to the positive charge.
Negative ion: add electrons equal to the charge magnitude.
Configuration exponents must add to the correct electron count.
Noble-gas shorthand uses the preceding noble gas.
Example: Br has 35 electrons. Br: [Ar]4s2 3d10 4p5. Total: 18+2+10+5 = 35.
The same configuration may be written with 3d before 4s. Written order does not change occupancy.
TOPIC: CONFIGURATION EXCEPTIONS
Exceptions specifically named in the study guide:
Cr: [Ar]4s1 3d5. Mo: [Kr]5s1 4d5. Cu: [Ar]4s1 3d10. Ag: [Kr]5s1 4d10. Au: [Xe]6s1 4f14 5d10.
Cr and Mo have half-filled d subshells. Cu, Ag, and Au have filled d subshells.
Nearby s and d energies make these arrangements favorable.
“Half-filled or filled subshell stability” is a useful simplified explanation. Do not apply it as a universal rule that predicts every transition-metal configuration.
TOPIC: TRANSITION-METAL IONS
When forming these cations, remove outer ns electrons before electrons in the (n−1)d subshell.
For the first transition series, remove 4s before 3d.
Filling 4s before 3d does not mean removing 3d first.
Examples: Co: [Ar]4s2 3d7. Co2+: [Ar]3d7.
Fe: [Ar]4s2 3d6. Fe2+: [Ar]3d6. Fe3+: [Ar]3d5.
Cu: [Ar]4s1 3d10. Cu+: [Ar]3d10. Cu2+: [Ar]3d9.
Do not change an ion back to the neutral atom’s electron count when checking its magnetism.
TOPIC: VALENCE ELECTRONS
For main-group atoms, valence electrons occupy the outermost shell.
Group 1: 1 valence electron. Group 2: 2. Groups 13–18: 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8. Helium: 2.
Transition-metal chemistry can involve outer s and incomplete d subshells.
The course guide excludes filled d and f subshells from its valence-electron counting convention.
TOPIC: MAGNETISM
Diamagnetic: all electrons paired; weakly repelled by a magnetic field.
Paramagnetic: at least one unpaired electron; attracted by a magnetic field.
Check the actual configuration of the atom or ion.
Unpaired-electron counts using Hund filling for isolated atoms or ions:
s1: 1. s2: 0.
p1, p2, p3, p4, p5, p6: 1, 2, 3, 2, 1, 0.
d1, d2, d3, d4, d5, d6, d7, d8, d9, d10: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0.
Cu+ has d10: diamagnetic. Cu2+ has d9: paramagnetic.
A filled subshell has no unpaired electrons.
Ferromagnetism involves collective alignment of magnetic moments in certain materials. The study guide gives this lower priority.
TOPIC: LAST-ELECTRON QUANTUM NUMBERS
Identify the last occupied subshell.
Read n from the subshell number. Convert s, p, d, f to l = 0, 1, 2, 3. Draw the orbital boxes. Apply Hund and Pauli. Assign ml and ms using the class’s stated box order and spin convention.
An up arrow is commonly assigned ms = +1/2, but use the stated convention.
Without a specified convention, equivalent degenerate orbitals can allow different ml labels. Do not claim a unique box assignment when the question has not specified one.
CATEGORY 2: QUANTUM NUMBERS, ORBITALS, AND NODES
TOPIC: FOUR QUANTUM NUMBERS
n identifies the shell. Allowed n: 1, 2, 3, and larger positive integers.
l identifies subshell shape. Allowed l: integers from 0 through n−1.
l = 0 means s. l = 1 means p. l = 2 means d. l = 3 means f.
ml identifies orbital orientation. Allowed ml: integers from −l through +l.
ms identifies electron spin. Allowed ms: +1/2 or −1/2 only.
TOPIC: ALLOWED SETS
Every rule must be satisfied.
n = 2, l = 2 is impossible because l can only be 0 or 1.
l = 1, ml = 2 is impossible because ml must be −1, 0, or +1.
ms = 0 is impossible.
For l = 3, ml can be −3, −2, −1, 0, +1, +2, +3.
TOPIC: SHELL, SUBSHELL, ORBITAL
Shell: same n.
Subshell: same n and l.
Orbital: one spatial state specified by n, l, and ml.
Each orbital holds up to two electrons with opposite spins.
Orbitals in a subshell = 2l+1. Maximum electrons in a subshell = 2(2l+1).
s: 1 orbital, 2 electrons. p: 3 orbitals, 6 electrons. d: 5 orbitals, 10 electrons. f: 7 orbitals, 14 electrons.
Orbitals in a shell = n squared. Maximum electrons in a shell = 2n squared.
n = 1: 1 orbital, 2 electrons. n = 2: 4 orbitals, 8 electrons. n = 3: 9 orbitals, 18 electrons. n = 4: 16 orbitals, 32 electrons.
TOPIC: FIRST POSSIBLE SUBSHELL
s first occurs at n = 1. p first occurs at n = 2. d first occurs at n = 3. f first occurs at n = 4.
There is no 1p, 2d, or 3f subshell.
TOPIC: SHAPES AND PROBABILITY
s orbitals are spherical.
p orbitals have two lobes.
Most d orbitals have four lobes. The dz2 orbital has two lobes and a ring.
Orbital colors or plus/minus labels represent wavefunction phase, not positive and negative electric charge.
An orbital is not a fixed circular electron path.
|ψ| squared gives probability density.
Orbital drawings often enclose about 90% of the electron’s probability. That drawn boundary is not a hard wall.
TOPIC: NODES
A node has zero electron probability density.
Total nodes = n−1. Angular nodes = l. Radial nodes = n−l−1.
Angular nodes are not always flat planes. Some are conical.
To identify an orbital from nodes: Add angular and radial nodes. n = total nodes+1. l = angular nodes. Convert l to s, p, d, or f.
Examples: 3p: 2 total, 1 angular, 1 radial. 5p: 4 total, 1 angular, 3 radial. 5d: 4 total, 2 angular, 2 radial.
TOPIC: DEGENERACY
Degenerate means equal energy.
In the basic hydrogen model, orbitals with the same n have the same energy. Hydrogen 2s and 2p are degenerate.
In many-electron atoms, orbitals within the same subshell are degenerate in the usual isolated-atom model.
Many-electron 2s and 2p have different energies.
CATEGORY 3: PERIODIC TRENDS AND EXCEPTIONS
TOPIC: EFFECTIVE NUCLEAR CHARGE
Zeff is the effective positive nuclear charge felt by an electron.
The nucleus attracts electrons. Other electrons provide shielding.
A simple estimate subtracts shielding from nuclear charge. Using the number of core electrons as shielding is an approximation.
Across a period, Zeff generally increases.
Core electrons shield more effectively than electrons in the same shell.
Stronger effective attraction tends to pull electrons closer and make removal harder.
TOPIC: ATOMIC SIZE
Atomic radius generally increases down a group because additional shells are occupied.
Atomic radius generally decreases from left to right because effective nuclear attraction increases.
Largest atoms are generally toward the bottom left.
Do not assume a simple trend resolves every comparison between unrelated positions.
TOPIC: ION SIZE
Cations are smaller than their neutral parent atoms.
Anions are larger than their neutral parent atoms.
Losing an entire outer shell can produce a large size decrease.
Adding electrons increases electron-electron repulsion.
TOPIC: ISOELECTRONIC SERIES
Isoelectronic species have the same electron count.
Within an isoelectronic series: More protons means stronger attraction and smaller radius.
Largest to smallest: O2−, F−, Ne, Na+, Mg2+.
All five have 10 electrons. Their proton counts increase from 8 to 12.
TOPIC: IONIZATION ENERGY
Ionization energy is the energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion.
Removal requires energy. Ionization energy is positive and endothermic.
It generally increases up and to the right.
It generally decreases down and to the left.
TOPIC: IONIZATION EXCEPTIONS
B has lower first ionization energy than Be. Al has lower first ionization energy than Mg.
Reason: removing the first p electron is easier than removing an electron from the filled s subshell.
O has lower first ionization energy than N. S has lower first ionization energy than P.
Reason: paired-electron repulsion makes an electron easier to remove than from the half-filled p arrangement.
TOPIC: SUCCESSIVE IONIZATION
Successive ionization energies increase.
After all valence electrons are removed, the next removal takes a much larger amount of energy.
A large jump between the second and third ionization energies suggests two valence electrons for a main-group atom.
TOPIC: ELECTRON AFFINITY
Use the course’s energy-change convention: A gaseous atom gains an electron to form a gaseous negative ion.
Negative electron affinity means energy is released.
More negative means more energy released.
Positive electron affinity means energy is required.
Do not confuse “more negative electron affinity” with “larger positive ionization energy.”
Ionization energy removes an electron. Electron affinity describes adding one.
TOPIC: ELECTRON-AFFINITY EXCEPTIONS
Electron addition generally becomes more favorable toward the upper right, but this has important exceptions.
Halogens have strongly negative electron affinities.
Noble gases have unfavorable electron addition.
Group 2 atoms have filled s subshells, making addition relatively unfavorable.
Group 15 atoms have half-filled p subshells, making addition less favorable than a simple trend predicts.
Chlorine has a more negative electron affinity than fluorine.
Fluorine’s small orbital causes greater electron-electron repulsion.
A single periodic-table arrow cannot correctly rank every electron-affinity comparison.
TOPIC: METALLIC CHARACTER
Metals tend to lose electrons.
Metallic character generally increases down and to the left.
Nonmetallic character generally increases up and to the right.
CATEGORY 4: LIGHT AND PHOTOELECTRIC EFFECT
TOPIC: LIGHT VOCABULARY
λ means wavelength: distance between corresponding points on neighboring waves.
ν means frequency: cycles per second.
Frequency units are Hz, meaning per second.
v means particle speed, not frequency.
Amplitude is wave height.
A photon is a discrete packet of electromagnetic energy.
TOPIC: ELECTROMAGNETIC ORDER
From lowest frequency and photon energy to highest:
Radio. Microwave. Infrared. Visible. Ultraviolet. X-ray. Gamma.
Wavelength decreases in that order.
The course guide says to memorize the order, not numerical boundaries.
TOPIC: VISIBLE COLORS
From lowest photon energy to highest: Red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet.
Red has the longest visible wavelength and lowest visible frequency and photon energy.
Violet has the shortest visible wavelength and highest visible frequency and photon energy.
Infrared is beyond red at longer wavelengths.
Ultraviolet is beyond violet at shorter wavelengths.
TOPIC: VISIBLE SPECTRUM NM
Visible light spans roughly 400 to 700 nm.
Below 400 nm is ultraviolet (UV).
Above 700 nm is infrared (IR).
UV has higher energy than visible light.
IR has lower energy than visible light.
TOPIC: RELATIONSHIPS
Longer wavelength means lower frequency and photon energy.
Shorter wavelength means higher frequency and photon energy.
At fixed frequency, higher intensity does not increase energy per photon.
For monochromatic light, higher intensity means more photons arriving per unit area per unit time.
Use the wavelength-frequency and photon-energy equations on the equation sheet.
TOPIC: BLACKBODY RADIATION
Heated objects emit electromagnetic radiation.
As temperature rises, peak emission shifts toward shorter wavelengths.
Planck explained this behavior using quantized energy exchanges.
TOPIC: PHOTOELECTRIC THRESHOLD
Φ is the work function: minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the material.
Photon energy below Φ: no electron is emitted in the ordinary single-photon process.
Photon energy equal to Φ: threshold; maximum emitted kinetic energy is zero.
Photon energy above Φ: the extra energy becomes electron kinetic energy.
Threshold frequency is the minimum frequency that can produce emission.
Threshold wavelength is the maximum wavelength that can produce emission.
Higher frequency means shorter wavelength.
TOPIC: FREQUENCY VERSUS INTENSITY
Increasing frequency above threshold increases maximum electron kinetic energy.
Increasing intensity at fixed frequency above threshold increases the number of emitted electrons, not their maximum kinetic energy.
Below threshold, increasing intensity does not produce ordinary single-photon emission.
TOPIC: CALCULATION CLUES
For emitted electron speed: Find photon energy. Subtract the work function to find maximum kinetic energy. Use the kinetic-energy equation to solve for speed.
A negative calculated kinetic energy means the photon is below threshold; no emission occurs.
Use meters for wavelength. Use Hz for frequency. Use joules for energy. Use kilograms for mass.
If energy is given in electron volts, convert to joules before using SI equations.
Use constants provided on the equation sheet. If absent, the electron-volt conversion is 1 eV = 1.602E−19 J.
CATEGORY 5: BOHR MODEL AND ATOMIC SPECTRA
TOPIC: SPECTRUM TYPES
Continuous spectrum: a continuous range of wavelengths.
Emission spectrum: bright lines at wavelengths emitted by atoms.
Absorption spectrum: dark lines where particular wavelengths were absorbed.
Each element has a characteristic spectral pattern.
An element absorbs and emits corresponding transition wavelengths.
TOPIC: ENERGY LEVELS
Ground state is the lowest allowed energy state.
Excited states have higher allowed energies.
Absorption moves an electron to a higher energy level.
Emission moves an electron to a lower energy level.
The absorbed or emitted photon matches the energy gap.
TOPIC: ENERGY SIGNS
Atomic energy change means final energy minus initial energy.
Absorption: positive atomic energy change.
Emission: negative atomic energy change.
Photon energy is always positive.
Use the magnitude of the atomic energy change when calculating photon wavelength or frequency.
Never report a negative photon wavelength.
TOPIC: INITIAL AND FINAL LEVELS
ni is the initial level. nf is the final level.
Emission: nf is lower than ni.
Absorption: nf is higher than ni.
Check the stated transition before substituting into an equation.
A larger energy gap gives higher-frequency, shorter-wavelength light.
A smaller gap gives lower-frequency, longer-wavelength light.
TOPIC: BOUND ENERGY AND IONIZATION
Bound-state energies are negative relative to a separated electron and nucleus.
Higher n corresponds to energy closer to zero.
Ionization takes the electron out of the bound atom.
For ionization, final n approaches infinity, so its inverse-square term becomes zero.
An electron starting at higher n requires less ionization energy.
TOPIC: HYDROGEN SERIES
Emission ending at n = 1: Lyman series; ultraviolet.
Emission ending at n = 2: Balmer series; visible and near-ultraviolet.
Emission ending at n = 3: Paschen series; infrared.
TOPIC: MODEL LIMITS
Bohr’s model works for hydrogen and other one-electron species, such as He+ and Li2+.
It does not correctly describe general many-electron atoms.
Use the nuclear-charge factor when the equation sheet calls for a one-electron ion rather than hydrogen.
@SIGNNOTE
TOPIC: OBSERVED COLOR
Emission can produce the color of a glowing source or atomic emission line.
Ordinary objects can also appear colored because they reflect or transmit some wavelengths and absorb others.
Do not claim every observed color is emitted light.
CATEGORY 6: MATTER WAVES AND UNCERTAINTY
TOPIC: MATTER WAVES
Moving matter has wave properties.
Use the de Broglie equation on the equation sheet.
Wavelength decreases as momentum increases.
At fixed speed, greater mass gives shorter wavelength.
At fixed mass, greater speed gives shorter wavelength.
A large everyday object has an extremely short wavelength, making its wave behavior difficult to notice.
Use kilograms and meters per second.
TOPIC: UNCERTAINTY
Position uncertainty describes how precisely position is specified.
Momentum uncertainty describes how precisely momentum is specified.
Smaller position uncertainty requires larger momentum uncertainty.
The uncertainty principle is a fundamental limit, not merely a consequence of bad equipment.
For fixed mass, momentum uncertainty can be related to speed uncertainty.
Use the uncertainty equation on the equation sheet.
If asked for the minimum uncertainty, use equality at the limit.
CATEGORY 7: SCIENTISTS AND EXPERIMENTS
TOPIC: EXPERIMENT TRIGGERS
Nucleus small, dense, +: Rutherford (gold foil).
Negative particles, e-: Thomson (cathode ray).
Oil drop, e- charge: Millikan (oil drop).
Radioactivity, atoms emit: Curie (radiation study).
Neutral particle, mass=p: Chadwick (neutron).
Blackbody, energy packets: Planck (quantized E).
Photoelectric effect: Einstein (photon theory).
Dark lines in sunlight: Fraunhofer (solar spec.).
Spectral lines by element: Bunsen & Kirchhoff.
Fixed mass proportions: Proust (definite prop.).
Whole-number mass ratios: Dalton (multiple prop.).
TOPIC: GREEK ATOMISTS
METHOD: Philosophical reasoning about matter.
PROPOSED: Matter consists of atoms.
LEARNED: An early atomic idea, not an experimental discovery.
Many other Greeks favored earth, air, fire, and water as the basic constituents.
TOPIC: BOYLE
METHOD: Chemical observations and experiments challenged traditional explanations.
OBSERVED: The four-element idea did not adequately explain chemical behavior.
LEARNED: Helped develop the modern idea of elements; explanations should be tested by observation and experiment.
TOPIC: LAVOISIER
METHOD: Used accurate balances and sealed containers to study combustion.
OBSERVED: Burning metal gained mass by combining with oxygen; total sealed-system mass remained constant.
LEARNED: Oxygen participates in combustion; mass is conserved.
Mass before a chemical reaction equals mass after, for a closed system.
TOPIC: PROUST
METHOD: Compared the composition of compound samples.
OBSERVED: A pure compound contained the same elements in fixed mass proportions.
LEARNED: Law of definite proportions.
TOPIC: DALTON
METHOD: Compared different compounds containing the same two elements.
OBSERVED: For a fixed mass of one element, masses of the other occurred in small whole-number ratios.
EXAMPLE: Oxygen masses in CO and CO2 have a 1:2 ratio for the same carbon mass.
LEARNED: Law of multiple proportions supports atomic theory.
DALTON’S THEORY: Matter consists of atoms. Different elements have different atoms. Compounds contain atoms in whole-number ratios. Chemical reactions rearrange atoms.
Later discoveries revised the claims that atoms are indivisible and that every atom of one element has identical mass.
TOPIC: THOMSON
METHOD: Studied cathode rays and their deflection by electric and magnetic fields.
OBSERVED: The beam bent toward a positive plate.
LEARNED: The beam contained negative particles, electrons.
Measured electron charge-to-mass ratio.
Electrons occurred in different materials, showing atoms contain smaller particles.
Proposed plum-pudding model: electrons embedded in diffuse positive matter.
TOPIC: MILLIKAN AND FLETCHER
METHOD: Observed charged oil droplets in an electric field that opposed gravity.
OBSERVED: Droplet charges were multiples of one basic charge.
LEARNED: Measured electron charge.
Electron mass was calculated by combining this charge with Thomson’s charge-to-mass ratio.
Do not say the oil-drop experiment independently measured electron mass.
TOPIC: CURIE
METHOD: Studied radiation from radioactive materials.
OBSERVED: Radioactivity was associated with atomic properties; identified polonium and radium with Pierre Curie.
LEARNED: Atoms can undergo changes beyond ordinary chemical reactions.
Do not credit Curie with discovering the proton or every radiation type.
TOPIC: RUTHERFORD
METHOD: Directed positive alpha particles at thin gold foil.
OBSERVED: Most passed through; some deflected; very few turned back.
LEARNED: Atoms are mostly empty space with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus containing most atomic mass.
Disproved the diffuse positive-charge plum-pudding model.
Alpha particles are helium nuclei, not single protons.
TOPIC: CHADWICK
METHOD: Bombarded beryllium with alpha particles; examined neutral radiation that knocked protons out of paraffin.
OBSERVED: Collision behavior indicated neutral particles with approximately proton mass.
LEARNED: Discovered the neutron.
Neutrons explain much of atomic mass and isotope mass differences.
Isotopes were known before Chadwick’s discovery.
TOPIC: PLANCK
METHOD: Theoretical explanation of blackbody radiation.
EXPLAINED: Energy exchanges occur in discrete amounts.
LEARNED: Energy is quantized; packet energy depends on frequency.
TOPIC: EINSTEIN
METHOD: Theoretical explanation of the photoelectric effect.
EXPLAINED: One photon transfers energy to one electron; emission requires sufficient photon energy.
LEARNED: Light has particle behavior. Excess photon energy becomes electron kinetic energy.
TOPIC: FRAUNHOFER
METHOD: Examined the solar spectrum.
OBSERVED: Dark lines at particular wavelengths.
LEARNED: Some wavelengths are missing from sunlight reaching us.
TOPIC: BUNSEN AND KIRCHHOFF
METHOD: Examined spectra of heated elements and compared emission and absorption.
OBSERVED: Elements had characteristic spectral lines; corresponding wavelengths could be emitted and absorbed.
LEARNED: Spectra identify elements and explain missing wavelengths caused by absorption.
TOPIC: BALMER AND RYDBERG
METHOD: Analyzed hydrogen spectral wavelengths mathematically.
OBSERVED: Wavelength patterns followed relationships involving whole numbers.
LEARNED: Hydrogen spectral lines could be predicted.
TOPIC: BOHR
METHOD: Proposed quantized electron energy levels to explain hydrogen spectra.
EXPLAINED: Electrons absorb or emit photons when changing allowed levels.
LEARNED: Discrete levels explain hydrogen’s line spectrum.
TOPIC: DE BROGLIE
METHOD: Theoretical proposal extending wave-particle behavior to matter.
PROPOSED: Moving particles have a wavelength related to momentum.
LEARNED: Matter has wave properties; later diffraction experiments supported this.
TOPIC: HEISENBERG
METHOD: Developed quantum-mechanical uncertainty relationships.
ESTABLISHED: Exact position and momentum cannot both be specified simultaneously.
LEARNED: A fundamental limit applies to their combined precision.
TOPIC: SCHRODINGER
METHOD: Developed a wave equation for electron states.
ESTABLISHED: Solutions describe allowed energies and spatial wavefunctions.
LEARNED: Probability density replaces fixed electron paths in the orbital description.
TOPIC: ALPHA, BETA, GAMMA
Alpha: helium nucleus; +2 charge; relatively low penetrating power.
Beta-minus: emitted electron; −1 charge; generally more penetrating than alpha.
Gamma: high-energy photon; no charge; highly penetrating.
Radioactive decay does not always split an atom into two large pieces.
CATEGORY 8: MOLES, MASSES, AND ISOTOPES
TOPIC: THREE MASS TERMS
Mass number: whole-number count of protons plus neutrons.
Atomic mass: isotope mass or isotope-weighted average, depending on context.
Periodic-table atomic mass: average over naturally occurring isotopes.
Molar mass: mass per mole, in grams per mole.
An element’s atomic mass in amu and molar mass in grams per mole have numerically corresponding values at this course’s precision.
One amu is one-twelfth the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
TOPIC: ISOTOPE AVERAGES
Multiply each isotope’s mass by its fractional abundance, then add.
Convert percent abundance to a decimal first.
All fractional abundances sum to 1. All percentages sum to 100%.
The average lies between the isotope masses.
For two isotopes, if one fraction is x, the other is 1−x.
A more abundant isotope pulls the average closer to its mass.
TOPIC: NATURAL ABUNDANCE
Two-isotope formula:
Avg = (M1*x) + (M2*(1-x))
x = fraction of isotope 1.
x*100 = percent of isotope 1.
1-x = fraction of isotope 2.
Direct solve for x:
x = (Avg - M2) / (M1 - M2)
Sanity check: Avg is always closest to the mass of the most abundant isotope.
TOPIC: MASS SPECTROMETRY
Mass spectrometry separates ions according to mass-to-charge ratio.
It helps determine isotope masses and relative abundances.
Do not describe it as measuring mass alone without considering charge.
TOPIC: MOLES
A mole is a particle-counting unit, like a very large dozen.
One mole contains approximately 6.022E23 specified particles.
Specify whether the particles are atoms, molecules, or formula units.
Use the Avogadro constant supplied on the exam.
TOPIC: CONVERSION WORKFLOW
Grams to moles: divide by molar mass.
Moles to grams: multiply by molar mass.
Moles to particles: multiply by Avogadro’s constant.
Particles to moles: divide by Avogadro’s constant.
For grams to particles, go through moles.
For compound molar mass, multiply each element’s atomic mass by its subscript, then add.
TOPIC: COUNTING ATOMS IN COMPOUNDS
One mole of H2O molecules contains: Two moles of hydrogen atoms. One mole of oxygen atoms. Three moles of atoms total.
Subscripts count atoms per molecule; they do not change the number of molecules.
Use parentheses correctly when a subscript multiplies an entire group.
TOPIC: MASS PERCENT
Mass percent means component mass divided by total mass, times 100.
For an element in a compound, compare that element’s contribution to the compound’s molar mass with the total molar mass.
Mass percent is not the same as atom-count percent.
TOPIC: LAWS OF COMPOSITION
Law of definite proportions: A compound always contains the same elements in the same mass ratio.
Water always has about 8 grams of oxygen for every 1 gram of hydrogen.
Law of multiple proportions: When two elements form different compounds, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other have a small whole-number ratio.
In CO and CO2, 12 grams of carbon combines with 16 or 32 grams of oxygen. That oxygen ratio is 1:2.
A related law is conservation of mass: In a chemical reaction, matter is rearranged. Total mass before the reaction equals total mass afterward.
CATEGORY 9: ATOMS, IONS, AND CHARGES
TOPIC: SUBATOMIC PARTICLES
Proton: positive charge; nucleus; approximately 1 amu.
Neutron: no charge; nucleus; approximately 1 amu.
Electron: negative charge; outside nucleus; much smaller mass, approximately 1/1836 of proton mass.
Most atomic mass is in the nucleus.
TOPIC: PARTICLE COUNTS
Atomic number Z = number of protons.
The proton count determines the element.
Mass number A = protons plus neutrons.
Neutrons = A−Z.
Neutral atom: electrons equal protons.
Positive ion: fewer electrons than protons.
Negative ion: more electrons than protons.
Signed charge = protons minus electrons.
TOPIC: EXAMPLES
Ca has 20 protons. Ca2+ has 18 electrons.
Cl has 17 protons. Cl− has 18 electrons.
Changing electrons changes charge, not element identity.
Changing protons changes the element.
Changing neutrons changes the isotope.
TOPIC: ISOTOPES VERSUS IONS
Isotopes of one element have the same protons and different neutrons.
Ions have a net charge because proton and electron counts differ.
A species is not an ion merely because it appears in an isoelectronic list.
Neutral Ar has no net charge.
TOPIC: ELECTROSTATIC INTERACTIONS
Like charges repel.
Opposite charges attract.
Larger charge magnitudes increase force magnitude.
Greater separation decreases force magnitude.
Coulomb force follows an inverse-square distance relationship.
CATEGORY 10: MEASUREMENT AND BASIC MATTER
TOPIC: SCIENTIFIC NOTATION
Scientific notation uses a coefficient with magnitude at least 1 and less than 10, multiplied by a power of ten.
Multiplication: multiply coefficients and add exponents.
Division: divide coefficients and subtract exponents.
A negative exponent indicates a small magnitude, not a negative value.
Calculator E notation represents a power of ten.
TOPIC: SIGNIFICANT FIGURES
Nonzero digits count.
Zeros between nonzero digits count.
Leading zeros do not count.
Trailing zeros after a decimal point count.
Trailing zeros in whole numbers without a decimal can be ambiguous. Scientific notation clarifies precision.
Examples: 0.00450 has 3 significant figures. 4.502 has 4. 3.900 has 4. 620. has 3. 6.20E2 has 3.
TOPIC: ROUNDING RULES
Multiply or divide: answer has the fewest significant figures among measured inputs.
Add or subtract: answer ends at the least precise decimal place.
Exact counts and defined conversion factors do not limit precision.
Keep extra digits during calculations. Round at the end.
A measured value contains certain digits plus one estimated digit.
TOPIC: METRIC PREFIXES
Each entry gives the number of base units in one prefixed unit.
exa, E: 10^18. peta, P: 10^15. tera, T: 10^12. giga, G: 10^9. mega, M: 10^6. kilo, k: 10^3. hecto, h: 10^2. deka, da: 10^1. base unit: 10^0. deci, d: 10^−1. centi, c: 10^−2. milli, m: 10^−3. micro, μ: 10^−6. nano, n: 10^−9. pico, p: 10^−12. femto, f: 10^−15. atto, a: 10^−18.
Capitalization matters: M is mega; m is milli.
TOPIC: COMMON CONVERSION TRAPS
1 nm = 1E−9 m. 1 MHz = 1E6 Hz. 1 ms = 1E−3 s. 1 g = 1E−3 kg. 1 cm3 = 1 mL. 1 L = 1000 mL.
Hz means per second. ms means milliseconds.
Cancel unwanted units using conversion factors.
Square conversion factors for area. Cube conversion factors for volume.
Use density to connect mass and volume.
Kelvin temperature is Celsius temperature plus 273.15.
TOPIC: MATTER CLASSES
Matter has mass and occupies space.
Element: one type of atom.
Compound: different elements chemically combined in fixed proportions.
Molecule: two or more chemically bonded atoms.
Not every compound consists of separate molecules.
Pure substance: element or compound with fixed composition.
Mixture: substances physically combined in variable proportions.
Homogeneous mixture: uniform throughout.
Heterogeneous mixture: not uniform throughout.
Mixtures can be separated physically. Separating a compound into its elements requires chemical change.
TOPIC: PROPERTIES AND CHANGES
Physical property: observed without changing chemical identity. Examples: density, color, melting point, conductivity.
Chemical property: ability to undergo chemical change. Examples: flammability, reaction with acid.
Physical change: chemical identity stays the same. Examples: melting, boiling, cutting.
Chemical change: new substances form. Examples: burning, rusting.
Intensive property: independent of sample amount. Examples: density, temperature.
Extensive property: depends on sample amount. Examples: mass, volume, total energy.
TOPIC: STATES OF MATTER
Solid: fixed shape and volume.
Liquid: fixed volume; takes container shape.
Gas: takes container shape and volume.
TOPIC: PHASE CHANGES
Melting: solid to liquid. Freezing: liquid to solid. Vaporization: liquid to gas. Condensation: gas to liquid. Sublimation: solid to gas. Deposition: gas to solid.
Melting, vaporization, and sublimation absorb energy.
Freezing, condensation, and deposition release energy.
Phase changes are physical changes.
TOPIC: ENERGY
Kinetic energy: energy of motion.
Potential energy: energy associated with position or arrangement.
Radiant energy: energy carried by electromagnetic radiation.
