CATEGORY 7: SCIENTISTS AND EXPERIMENTS

GREEK ATOMISTS
METHOD: Philosophical reasoning about matter.
PROPOSED: Matter consists of atoms.
LEARNED: An early atomic idea, not an experimental discovery.
Many other Greeks favored earth, air, fire, and water as the basic constituents.

BOYLE
METHOD: Chemical observations and experiments challenged traditional explanations.
OBSERVED: The four-element idea did not adequately explain chemical behavior.
LEARNED: Helped develop the modern idea of elements; explanations should be tested by observation and experiment.

LAVOISIER
METHOD: Used accurate balances and sealed containers to study combustion.
OBSERVED: Burning metal gained mass by combining with oxygen; total sealed-system mass remained constant.
LEARNED: Oxygen participates in combustion; mass is conserved.
Mass before a chemical reaction equals mass after, for a closed system.

PROUST
METHOD: Compared the composition of compound samples.
OBSERVED: A pure compound contained the same elements in fixed mass proportions.
LEARNED: Law of definite proportions.

DALTON
METHOD: Compared different compounds containing the same two elements.
OBSERVED: For a fixed mass of one element, masses of the other occurred in small whole-number ratios.
EXAMPLE: Oxygen masses in CO and CO2 have a 1:2 ratio for the same carbon mass.
LEARNED: Law of multiple proportions supports atomic theory.
DALTON’S THEORY: Matter consists of atoms. Different elements have different atoms. Compounds contain atoms in whole-number ratios. Chemical reactions rearrange atoms.
Later discoveries revised the claims that atoms are indivisible and that every atom of one element has identical mass.

THOMSON
METHOD: Studied cathode rays and their deflection by electric and magnetic fields.
OBSERVED: The beam bent toward a positive plate.
LEARNED: The beam contained negative particles, electrons.
Measured electron charge-to-mass ratio.
Electrons occurred in different materials, showing atoms contain smaller particles.
Proposed plum-pudding model: electrons embedded in diffuse positive matter.

MILLIKAN AND FLETCHER
METHOD: Observed charged oil droplets in an electric field that opposed gravity.
OBSERVED: Droplet charges were multiples of one basic charge.
LEARNED: Measured electron charge.
Electron mass was calculated by combining this charge with Thomson’s charge-to-mass ratio.
Do not say the oil-drop experiment independently measured electron mass.

CURIE
METHOD: Studied radiation from radioactive materials.
OBSERVED: Radioactivity was associated with atomic properties; identified polonium and radium with Pierre Curie.
LEARNED: Atoms can undergo changes beyond ordinary chemical reactions.
Do not credit Curie with discovering the proton or every radiation type.

RUTHERFORD
METHOD: Directed positive alpha particles at thin gold foil.
OBSERVED: Most passed through; some deflected; very few turned back.
LEARNED: Atoms are mostly empty space with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus containing most atomic mass.
Disproved the diffuse positive-charge plum-pudding model.
Alpha particles are helium nuclei, not single protons.

CHADWICK
METHOD: Bombarded beryllium with alpha particles; examined neutral radiation that knocked protons out of paraffin.
OBSERVED: Collision behavior indicated neutral particles with approximately proton mass.
LEARNED: Discovered the neutron.
Neutrons explain much of atomic mass and isotope mass differences.
Isotopes were known before Chadwick’s discovery.

PLANCK
METHOD: Theoretical explanation of blackbody radiation.
EXPLAINED: Energy exchanges occur in discrete amounts.
LEARNED: Energy is quantized; packet energy depends on frequency.

EINSTEIN
METHOD: Theoretical explanation of the photoelectric effect.
EXPLAINED: One photon transfers energy to one electron; emission requires sufficient photon energy.
LEARNED: Light has particle behavior. Excess photon energy becomes electron kinetic energy.

FRAUNHOFER
METHOD: Examined the solar spectrum.
OBSERVED: Dark lines at particular wavelengths.
LEARNED: Some wavelengths are missing from sunlight reaching us.

BUNSEN AND KIRCHHOFF
METHOD: Examined spectra of heated elements and compared emission and absorption.
OBSERVED: Elements had characteristic spectral lines; corresponding wavelengths could be emitted and absorbed.
LEARNED: Spectra identify elements and explain missing wavelengths caused by absorption.

BALMER AND RYDBERG
METHOD: Analyzed hydrogen spectral wavelengths mathematically.
OBSERVED: Wavelength patterns followed relationships involving whole numbers.
LEARNED: Hydrogen spectral lines could be predicted.

BOHR
METHOD: Proposed quantized electron energy levels to explain hydrogen spectra.
EXPLAINED: Electrons absorb or emit photons when changing allowed levels.
LEARNED: Discrete levels explain hydrogen’s line spectrum.

DE BROGLIE
METHOD: Theoretical proposal extending wave-particle behavior to matter.
PROPOSED: Moving particles have a wavelength related to momentum.
LEARNED: Matter has wave properties; later diffraction experiments supported this.

HEISENBERG
METHOD: Developed quantum-mechanical uncertainty relationships.
ESTABLISHED: Exact position and momentum cannot both be specified simultaneously.
LEARNED: A fundamental limit applies to their combined precision.

SCHRODINGER
METHOD: Developed a wave equation for electron states.
ESTABLISHED: Solutions describe allowed energies and spatial wavefunctions.
LEARNED: Probability density replaces fixed electron paths in the orbital description.

ALPHA, BETA, GAMMA
Alpha: helium nucleus; +2 charge; relatively low penetrating power.
Beta-minus: emitted electron; −1 charge; generally more penetrating than alpha.
Gamma: high-energy photon; no charge; highly penetrating.
Radioactive decay does not always split an atom into two large pieces.
